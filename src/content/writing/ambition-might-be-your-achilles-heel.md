I gave this talk at TEDxGeorgiaTech in November 2023, in my fourth year at Georgia Tech, with no public speaking behind me. This is the final draft, lightly edited for reading.

Do you ever have those moments where you stop and think to yourself: “WHAT am I doing?”

For example:

Why am I pursuing a degree that my parents wanted me to?

Why am I committed to so many extracurriculars that don’t matter to me?

What the hell am I doing up here giving a TED Talk?

These moments are absolutely critical to us being able to transform our lives. Some of these moments are more important than others, and these reflections allow us to make changes to help us answer one central question:

> Am I becoming who I want to become?

I am here tonight to share some of my biggest “what the hell” realizations in regards to my ambition in hopes that I can help you answer this question for yourself.

My name is Austin Rowland, and I am a fourth year undergrad student here at Georgia Tech.

I am a dedicated engineer, an RA, a content creator, an assistive technology product developer, and more depending on who you ask. All of these tie into my underlying foundation of being someone who wants to dedicate his life to helping other people.

I have always wanted to leave behind a massive impact on the world, even before I knew exactly which avenues I wanted to pursue to make that a reality.

Some of my future goals include:
- By 25, I will have launched at least one company or widespread product
- By 30, I will directly change 100K people’s lives with all my avenues of work
- By 50, I will be proof of how much one person can change the world

All of these goals are the result of my journey over 4 years here at Georgia Tech, which resulted in my ambition growing to levels that I never could have imagined.

My continued growth into more ambitious goals and plans for my life has helped me get closer and closer to the person I want to become; however, it has not been a linear process. In a counterintuitive way, the larger my ambition got, the larger my problems became as well.

Ambition is a miraculous thing that has been both my guiding light and my achilles heel. By reflecting upon the setbacks I experienced, I realized my ambition was also holding me back; and in sharing these lessons I hope to jump start your journey to finding just the right level of ambition.

## Lesson 1: Ambition naturally attracts other ambitious people, leverage this to the fullest.

My first year at Georgia Tech was a huge shift for me.

Being from the small town of Walkersville, MD, I grew up immersed in competition where I was a top performer my whole life. On top of that, I had a very competitive friend group. Everything became a competition with them: grades, sports, who could impress the most girls. And for most of my life, I thrived with competition when I felt I was on the same level or doing better than others, except for the girls part.

When I came to college, I began comparing myself to the sea of incredibly brilliant people that is Georgia Tech. I struggled in school and called home almost every day thinking I was going to fail out of my classes and transfer schools while others excelled at classes. I was surrounded by people that had coded since they were 8 and launched nonprofits in high school, how could I ever compete at this school?

Since I was friends with so many amazing people, I got to know them better as time went on. They talked about their lives and how they felt being at a school like Georgia Tech. I noticed many of them had regrets about how they spent their time, and envied things about my life as well. They wished they had spent more time focusing on sports and friends, or taking more time to enjoy their youth. How could all these impressive people envy me?

I stopped comparing myself to others because what I realized is that every person has different starting points and wildly different timelines for how things play out. No one was better than me, they were just different from me. I stopped putting myself in the rooms I had thought I belonged in, and I started putting myself in rooms that challenged me to be better. I finally started seeing how we are a collective and we are all on this journey together.

Being surrounded by ambitious people can be viewed as a source of competition, but what it should be viewed as is a source of inspiration. Allow this environment to elevate you towards your goals and give you motivation by seeing all that others are capable of, rather than thinking about how far behind you are. See others' success as evidence that you can do it too.

## Lesson 2: Ambition can become a deterrent without focus and discipline.

Seeing the success of others around me helped me to continue developing more and more ambitious goals. Once I started to dream bigger, I ran into yet another obstacle: the confidence of knowing I could do anything made me want to do a million things.

During my sophomore year, I had plans that fell through with my money for school. I was terrified; I thought I was going to have to drop out of school and give up all that I had worked so hard for. Before really having time to process anything, I immediately got to work because I had no choice. I had never worked so hard in my life, with most weeks being 100 hours between my Blue Donkey job, my RA job, and 5 of the hardest STEM courses I’ve ever taken. I was on the edge of dropping out of my studies and joining the military, which weighed on me for so many months.

After my helluva semester and my summer job, I had finally made it. I was able to financially support myself after all my hard work. Dropping out was no longer something I had to stress over 24/7 and now I had all this time to fill. This gave me the confidence to really start dreaming bigger. If I could make it through this, then I could really do anything.

In my junior year, I started signing up for a million things as my ambitions started to grow. I wanted to be an engineer, product designer, startup founder, content creator, nonprofit founder, photographer, model, boxer, and so much more! And I did what any normal person would do, I began working on all of these at the same time! If you ask people here in any of these communities, there’s a good chance they know who I am.

But I always felt like I never had the time to dedicate towards becoming great at any one thing from my list. I started around the same time as my other friends trying to accomplish these same goals. By the end of the year, I made little progress, but I had friends that were taking pictures for celebrities, launching their own startups and nonprofits, and getting offers for their dream jobs.

I realized that by trying to accomplish so much, I was spreading myself thin. It was a no-brainer that my friends were becoming much more accomplished in their fields; I was trying to do all of these at once while we all have the same 24 hours in a day. It was amazing for me to want to do all of these things (as it helped me discover the things I was truly interested in), but I needed to prioritize so that I could dedicate the time needed to become great at one or two things before moving onto the next. I started becoming more intentional with where I invested my time, and making sure that I was making the progress I wanted to propel me into my future.

One lesson I learned was that you CAN do anything, but you CAN’T do everything, especially not at the same time.

You can continue to push the bounds of what you can do so much by constantly experimenting and switching gears to bigger challenges, but too much and too often can prevent you from making any tangible progress in any one thing.

Your ambition will help broaden your horizons, but it is your discipline that will move you towards them.

## Lesson 3: Ambition and humanity need not be exclusive.

For the last 5 or 6 years, I have always put so much pressure on myself to always maximize my productivity.

My life has been rather cyclical, always oscillating between happy, productive periods and draining, unproductive periods. It was frustrating because no matter how good I was doing or how much I was accomplishing, I always ended up returning to months of stagnation every year.

Every time I entered an “unproductive” period, I always got down and told myself over and over about how I wasn’t doing enough. My own negative self-talk started to drain the very energy I needed to be accomplishing the things I wanted to be accomplishing, which ultimately set up these negative feedback loops in my life.

After talking to dozens of friends and residents, I realized a common problem people face: the pressure to always be doing more while your own negative self-perception drags you down. Many people face these negative feedback loops, and the most important thing to do when you are stuck in the cycle is to realize and take action.

Now when things get tough, I hit my reset button. I stop worrying about all the things that propel me towards my goals, and I immediately focus on the things that make me happy in the present, like hitting the gym, reading books, and seeing friends. I went from viewing them as “unproductive” periods to rest periods where I could find grounding in the whirlwind that is my life.

Gratitude is an amazing tool to pull yourself out of a negative feedback loop. Rather than always focusing on the future, having gratitude for where you are and where you came from can help you to feel more motivated for where you are going.

You can have big dreams without putting the weight of the world on your shoulders. Contrary to popular belief, you do not need to be a machine or superman to find great success. It is okay to take breaks and embrace rest periods to care for yourself. You don’t need to forego your humanity in order to be successful. You need to embrace it.

## Am I becoming the person I want to become?

Ambition was my own experience, and I want to give you all the central toolkit that applies far beyond the topic of ambition.

Self reflection is the underlying foundation of this talk and this event’s theme. You cannot evolve into a better person without self reflection. It allowed me to determine that my ambition, which was helping push me forward, was also holding me back more than I realized. Self reflection allows us to correct course when we are headed in a different direction than where we want to go.

I want to use planes for an analogy here. Fun fact:

> “A plane is off course 90% of the time.” — Stephen R. Covey

Yet they still reach their destination. Why? Because they correct their course. You can too.

By continually taking the time to reflect on your direction, you can remain on your optimal life path. This also requires building in the time to self reflect. Some of the most brilliant people I know will crash and burn from overworking themselves, or end up in a totally different position than where they wanted to be, simply because they do not ever make enough time to stop and think about their own life. Building in even just 5 minutes a day to close out all other stressors can compound results quickly.

By adjusting your trajectory to align with your ever-changing desires and circumstances, you ensure that you’ll always be on course to reach your dream destinations.

These are all lessons that I learned the hard way that affected months or years of my life. Most of us Georgia Tech students compare ourselves to others, take on far too many commitments, and stay being our own worst critic.

If you encounter other challenges along the way, approach these with self-reflection too. Ambition was my Achilles Heel, and I hope in listening to this talk, you can discover and eliminate yours. I have wanted to say this my whole life. My name is Austin Rowland, and thank you for listening to my TED Talk.
